Disclaimers (Because I Have To)


First: This is objectively the right path. Am I saying you're going to read this and hit $10M/year? Absolutely not. Literally zero chance. Hitting 8 figures is a 3-5 year journey, and you're going to mess up a bunch. This is just the path.

Second: This is a general overview. There's a thousand ways to do it, and you're 1,000,000% going to run into roadblocks completely different than anything here.

Fourth: The best path is to scale what works and then pivot. If nothing's working, then maybe pivot. But every single info business ever tries a bunch of shit, it fails, one thing works (probably not what you expected), you scale the living shit out of that until it breaks, then you fix it, then you pivot.

Fifth: This is fully unbiased. I'm not trying to sell you anything, so I'm not going to tell you Facebook ads are dead because I want to sell you an organic course or vice versa.

Sixth: This doesn't cover all the infrastructure you need. You will need a sales team. You will need systems. This is what to do, not necessarily how to do every piece.

Finally: Start with being good at something. Crazy concept, I know. Selling education where you actually need to be good at the thing you're teaching.

Ideally, you're good enough that people are already asking you for help. Every great bizop VSL has that "I was doing this thing, and then all my hometown friends asked me how to get started" story. That shouldn't be an angle – it should be the truth.





Phase 1: Building Your Foundation


Step 1: Start Building Your Brand Around What You Want to Teach
Faceless offers? Don't get me started. I know they work. I've run them up myself. But we're getting to 8 figures. We're building a legitimate online education company, not some scrappy offer.

You need a brand. Even if you launch fully cold (which I've done), people will Google you. Facebook page SEO is super slept on. People are going to click on your Instagram page, Google you, check you out. Especially for higher-ticket B2B offers.



Two best ways to build your brand:



1. YouTube (The MVP)

Start TWO YouTube channels

One lifestyle/POV channel (doesn't mean flexing – means being human)

One value-focused channel (nothing held back, give everything away)

The lifestyle channel isn't about renting Lambos. It's about people getting to know YOU. If I started one, I wouldn't show my lifestyle because it's boring – I sit at my desk all day. But I'd talk about things outside of business that interest me.

People buy from humans. If they're going to send you thousands of dollars, they want to know who you are.



2. Go on Other People's Podcasts Such a hack. Especially if you're charismatic. Look at channels like Starter Story on YouTube. Those pseudo-podcast, long-form interview style channels are crushing right now.

Start building your network. Don't cold DM "yo can I get on your podcast?" Nurture relationships. Be good enough at your thing that people WANT to interview you, not just because you have followers, but because you're interesting.



Pro tip: Make a soft VSL for YouTube. Either go super highly produced (cinematic vibe, no hard sell) or raw style (Loom video, no edits, Miro board). Write a VSL without the close and without an aggressive open. Like a value webinar – pre-handle objections, walk through transformation process, bridge to solution, but keep it open-ended.



Step 2: Clip Everything


Use Opus Clip, Riverside, whatever. It's like $20/month and does it for you.

Low-key growth hack: Ask to clip the podcasts you were on. "Hey, I'd love to chop this up and share with my audience. Free growth for you." Clip it, post it, tag them, try to get reposts, add them as a contributor on Instagram. Instant authority.





Phase 2: Market Research (The Step Nobody Does)


Once you have ~1,500 followers on IG and ~700 on YouTube, it's time for the most important step that nobody does.

When you're building an offer, you don't guess. It's crazy how many people ask me "what should I sell?" I don't know. I haven't watched 100 hours of your content. Go ask your audience.



Here's exactly what to do:



Get on calls with your audience (Yes, actual calls)

Send an email or post a link asking them to get on a call

Do 10, 15, 20 calls. 30 minutes, 15 minutes, an hour – who cares?

Use a fake name/burner email if you want ("head of ops" doing market research)


Ask them real questions:

What's going on?

What are you working on?

How is that going?

What are you struggling with?

What have you tried?

Why'd you try that?

Why didn't you try this?

How can I help?


Actually help them (Don't gatekeep)

Give them real value on the call

The goal is they get so much value they don't want to hang up


Record everything and extract data:

Upload all recordings to Claude/ChatGPT

Ask it to extract the biggest shared pain points

Qualify your audience (financially, urgency of problems)

Find out what problems they have and how much they'd pay to solve them



Pro move: At the end of each call, if you've done it well enough and uncovered real pain points:

"That's awesome, dude. That's super helpful. We'll definitely take that into consideration. Transparency – I feel really good about this call because we were already building something in that direction. I've taken a lot of great stuff from this call. A lot of people are in your similar position with similar problems. I have your email – if you're interested, I'll keep you updated."

Boom. Call set. Throw them in an auto-nurture campaign.





Phase 3: Launch Your First Real Offer


Launch Round 1: One-on-One Consulting
You're a guru now. Congrats.



Key decisions:

Charge for it if you can (if your niche has money and your experience justifies it)

If you're truly starting out with no experience, do it free (but don't be that guy who charges when you shouldn't)

Cap it at 10-20 spots

Make it ACTUALLY one-on-one (not group calls disguised as 1:1)

Give them your WhatsApp, Discord channel, whatever

Time cap it (90 days is good, 30-day or 6-week sprints work too)



Why time caps matter:

You don't want people in your community 3 years later asking the same questions

Timelines create urgency (would you rather have your tooth pulled in 6 minutes or 90 days?)



During the program:

Gather generalized video testimonials (but make them LIVE demos, not scripted bullshit)

Record all your calls

Download all chat transcriptions

Track results if it's direct bizop

Do exit interviews



What you're actually doing: You're not building a course. You're solving problems. Like a personal trainer who doesn't give everyone the same workout routine – you're working with their specific problems and adjusting on the fly.





Phase 4: Create Your Scalable Program


After your first cohort, you now have two things:

A program (based on what actually worked)

Money

Take all your recordings and transcriptions. Have Claude turn them into:

Course modules/lessons

FAQ sheets

A 7-day fast start guide

Evergreen content



The problems you ended up solving probably weren't the problems they came with. Maybe you planned to help with service delivery but everyone needed help with acquisition. Maybe your entire program's success came from one cold email template you gave them.

Whatever actually worked – that's your program now.





Phase 5: Scale With A Real Funnel


Launch to Organic First
Throughout all this, you should still be:

Creating content

Uploading student testimonials

Starting your own podcast

Clipping everything



Now launch your high-ticket program:

Application VSL funnel (super standard)

Raw, super high-value VSL (not overproduced bullshit)

Typeform embedded, Calendly embedded

Take your own sales calls (at least initially)



Why take your own calls?

Way higher close rates

No disconnect between marketing and sales

You understand if your product actually helps

You hear objections directly (and can handle them in your marketing)



Then Add Low-Ticket Ascension
I'm biased toward low-ticket to high-ticket ascension. Here's why:

Benefits:

Easier to do on a low budget

Liquidates more ad spend

Buying more customers at scale

300 customers at $27 > 6 customers at $4k (for data purposes)

More people to survey, email, call, split test

Structure:

$27-97 front end

No OTOs, bumps, or bonuses initially (if you're good at marketing)

Direct to book-a-call after purchase

"One time offer: Get on the phone with me. Let's shorten this timeline."

(Shoutout to Josh Gavin for this)





Phase 6: Transition to Cold Traffic
When to Go Cold
My barometer: When I'm confident the funnel doesn't have leaks from organic.

It's not just about KPIs (though track those). It's about:

Is the funnel good?

Are the pre-call assets there?

Is the VSL good?

Are there massive bottlenecks?



How to Launch Cold
For Low-Ticket:

One campaign, one ad set, 5 ads

$50-100/day to start

Broad targeting

Dialed CAPI (Conversion API - this is crucial)

Pro tip from Alen: Add up your front end + OTOs + bumps. That total (minus high ticket) should be your daily ad budget. If ads don't suck, someone will buy your entire funnel.



For High-Ticket Book-a-Call:

Way more capital intensive

Higher risk but higher reward



Critical: Feed Facebook Quality Data
The difference between having 10 buyers Facebook knows about vs 100 buyers is the biggest leap you'll make in cold acquisition. Period.

Don't warm the pixel with people you don't want

Don't give Facebook unqualified leads

No traffic campaigns

No click campaigns

Just sales

Show Facebook who's buying and say "go find me more of those."





Phase 7: Scale Until It Breaks


This is where people fuck up. They hit $40k/month and think "time to do webinars!"

No. Just scale the living shit out of this thing.

If it's working:

10x the organic (hire agencies, clipping teams, etc.)

Scale ads aggressively (that day if metrics hold)

Don't launch new funnels

Don't change anything except volume

I've seen people at $600k/month with garbage offers just by ramming ads. If you're making $40k/month profit, add a couple zeros before you pivot.





Phase 8: Fix Your Broken Offer


When it breaks (and it will), welcome to the offer desk.

Usually it's one of two things:

Your mechanism doesn't make sense anymore

Your sales systems are too weak to handle volume



Common fixes:

Go more upmarket (remove abrasive language, sell to richer people)

Better sales reps (Oxford shirts, not 17-year-old Zoomers)

Clearer timeframes and transformations

Less educational language, more done-for-you framing

Actually listen to sales call recordings





Phase 9: Build Your Moat


At this point, you're ready to build a real business:



For the Offer:
Launch call booking to cold (you have social proof now)

Break it again and fix it again

Join 3 biggest B2C programs in your niche (competitive research)

Join 3 biggest B2B programs/masterminds (for your sales model)



For the Brand:
Build visual identity

Own digital real estate (be everywhere)

Massive content team

PR agencies, Forbes features, morning TV

Scale organic, low-ticket, and high-ticket book-a-call



Build Your Moat Around:
Your unique mechanism (that updates with current events)

Your USP

NOT your personal brand or story (those die when people stop caring)





Phase 10: The End Game


Get your AOV to $100k+

You should be able to sell something for $1 and something for $100k. If you can't, your business isn't good enough or you're in the wrong market.



Options:

Backend mastermind ($50k+/year)

Private equity plays (buy your students' businesses)

Massive affiliate plays



Build for an exit (even though info rarely exits):

Clean books

Position away from your face

Look at Athletic Greens' transformation

Scale until you're rich or dead.





The Reality Check
Most people won't make it to $10M. Not because this path doesn't work, but because:

They skip market research

They guess instead of asking their audience

They launch new offers instead of scaling what works

They hire too late (or too early)

They stop taking their own calls

They chase shiny objects



This is the path. Whether you stay on it is up to you.
