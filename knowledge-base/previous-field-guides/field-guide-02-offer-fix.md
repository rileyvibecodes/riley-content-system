Field Guide 02: The 10 Minute Offer Fix Exercise
Intro

When I had to think of a witty tagline for WBYO, I came up with:

“What you sell is fine. How you sell it isn’t.”

I’ve heard better. But it’ll stick.

The idea behind it is summarizing my entire philosophy on offers.

The only thing that matters about the product is that it gets damn good results for your customers. It has nothing to do with how well the offer sells.

How you sell it (AKA the positioning, the framing, the messaging, does)

Example:

Which product sounds better:

We teach you how to make $10k/mo in High Ticket Sales in 90 days

XYZ is a sales rep placement agency. We partner with consulting firms doing $500k/year+ to assign and train elite sales reps into their businesses. We genuinely cannot keep up with the demand, and have more deals in our pipeline than we do sales reps able to take on $10k/mo+ positions. Given our extensive training protocols, we take complete beginners, transform them into elite sales reps, and place them on a deal in our pipeline in 90 days or less

Yes, option B is longer. But it also makes more sense. It’s clearer to understand. The “okay, but what’s in it for you? Where’s the catch?” is answered.

If your offer requires 3 paragraphs, a Miro board, and explaining “it’s complicated,” the market will punish you.

This week’s Field Guide gives you a 10 minute exercise I run with businesses that cleans all that up, and unlocks conversions fast.

We’re going to make your offer kindergarten-simple, without dumbing it down.

When you finish, you’ll have:

A one-line offer anyone can understand

A 3 sentence “what/why/how” explainer you can paste on your landing page, or add to your VSL

A plain English mechanism line that makes your offer feel inevitable

New hero copy, ad hooks, and VSL/TSL leads that convert better (because they’re crysta clear”

If you do this, you will immediately see lifts in clicks, time on page, booking rates, and close rates

Why this works

People buy what they understand. Confused visitors don’t convert

Clarity raises perceived value. When the path is obvious the price resistance drops

Simple travels farther. Affiliates, partners, and customers can repeat a simple offer. This is how offers go “viral” – if people can repeat it to their friends at lunch, then it can spread through word of mouth (we will talk about Kozmin’s idea of the Viral Sales Letter in a later Field Guide)

The Feynman principle: if you can’t explain it simply, you don’t really understand it. Richard Feynman’s entire teaching philosophy was clarity-as-proof-of-understanding. This exercise applies that to your business

The 10-Minute Test:

(Actually do this. I never do these “exercises” when I see them either, but trust me. Don’t do it in your head or do it later. Just do it after you read it)

Set a timer for 10 minutes.

Open a blank doc.

DON’T use jargon, acronyms, or “it depends.”

1. Write your current offer in 1 sentence (60 seconds)

Use this as a starting point:

“We help \[who] get \[result] in \[timeframe], without \[dreaded thing]”

Side note: This is how most people’s offers FINISH. I see this headline, cold email script, etc. literally everywhere. I’m pretty sure Hormozi popularized it in his $100M Offers book. It works, but it’s the STARTING POINT

If that takes you more than a minute, your offer is already too complicated.

1. Do the dinner table test (3 minutes)

Imagine you’re at your good friend’s house. Mom asks, “So what actually IS your business?”

Answer in three sentences, max:

What is it? One simple sentence

How does it work? Two steps, max

Why is it good? Plain, tangible upside

Rules:

No jargon. If a 13-year-old wouldn’t know the word, swap it

No humble brag “oh it’s complicated.” If you can’t explain it, the offer isn’t ready

No outcomes without mechanism. “We get you clients” is incomplete. Tell me “how”

Template:

What: “I run a \[type of thing] that helps \[who] \[do what specific thing]”

How: “We do it by \[plain-English mechanism in 1–2 steps]”

Why: “It’s good because \[short, concrete benefit: faster, cheaper, easier, safer, more certain]”

Strip to first principles (2 minutes)

If you don’t know the definition of first principles thinking, Google it. I’ll dive into it in another Field Guide

Now remove every word you wouldn’t use with a child:

Replace “acquisition” with “get customers”

Replace “conversion optimization” with “make more visitors buy”

Replace “pipeline” with “list of people we follow up with”

Replace “n8n workflows” with “automated action”

Replace “SaaS” with “software”

Replace “LTV/ARPU/CAC” with “how much each customer pays us,” “average per customer,” “what it costs to get a customer”

Note: Yes, I know, that’s too simple. if you’re in a high awareness, sophisticated market, maybe M\&A or legal, you will want to use the appropriate language. That isn’t the point of this exercise. We’ll do a separate Field Guide on market adjacent tonality. The goal of this is for YOU to understand your offer so well, and be able to explain it to the average person. Not to write your ad copy

1. Mechanism in a sentence (1 minute)

Write the mechanism like you’d explain dropshipping to your parents:

“When someone buys, we take their order and send it directly to the supplier, who ships it to the customer. We keep the difference.”

Package the outputs (1 minute) You should now have:

One-line offer

3-sentence what/how/why

Mechanism sentence

This can now serve as your foundation for everything you write. Whether ads, VSLs, headlines, etc.

A lot of people get into their particular business in weird, obscure ways. Maybe someone reaches out and asks to partner, maybe they fall into it, maybe they see an ad for a course and decide to try it out.

Whatever it is – people rarely start by sitting down, objectively looking at everything, and carefully constructing a good offer.

Most of the time, the plane is built on the way up.

With this, they get so used to running and gunning, solving problems as they pop up, that they don’t take 10 minutes to sit down, strip everything back, and realign with the CORE of their business.

Practical Applications:

Headlines:

I built an offer that was basically a funnel and CRM template for creators. I was initially running the more DWY angle, like “we’ll show you how to get sales from your Instagram.” But when I did this exercise, I realized the offer was actually DFY. “We’ll SHOW YOU” implies learning, it implies they need to WATCH, which makes perceived effort higher, which makes conversions drop (it was a low ticket offer).

As I did the exercise, it was actually a “Plug and Play” solution.

So, the headline became:

“2,317 Creators Used This $20 Plug‑n‑Profit Funnel to Make Their First Sale Online – Copy, Paste, and Launch a Self-Selling Sales Machine in 15 Minutes.”

“✓‎ ‎ Self-Selling Sales Machine ‎ ✓ ‎ 1‑Click Setup ‎ ✓ ‎ Templates Included ‎ ✓ ‎ Live in 15 Minutes”

Without the exercise, I would’ve never uncovered that fully DFY “Plug and Play” framing, which when running a mass market bizop offer (we wanted big TAM for the “creators” on IG), crushes.

Ads:

For ads, there are a few ways you can use this.

The most basic and obvious is just running straight mechanistic ad copy.

Example:

“If you’re a creator, you could have a fully plug and play system setup to sell your digital products or coaching in the next 15 minutes”

Or “We will give you a fully plug and play website and selling system to XYZ”

Or whatever we decide to run.

Another great way is, once you have your mechanism dialled, just plugging it in into existing frames or angles.

Example:

I saw a statistic last week that the average digital creator makes less than minimum wage (ridiculously fire cold traffic angle). So, now that the mechanism is dialled, its super easy to just antagonize and then bridge to solution. Call out painpoint (basically just say the statistic) then let them know they can fix it in the next 15 minutes

Emails:

I’ll have to do a whole separate Field Guide on emails because most people get them wrong.

But for campaigns, they almost function similar to ads (in my opinion) – I’m usually just trying to find new ways to reiterate the mechanism, or finding unique angles, news stories, current events, fun ideas etc. to open curiosity and agitate pain points, and then since the mechanism is dialled, super easy to CTA back to the VSL to learn more.

I’d probably rip an email to this list with the subject line “The 3-sentence offer that sells itself:” for this Field Guide. Notice how that’s just an extraction of the mechanism.

Bonus Tip: FAQ Section on Funnel

FAQs are so slept on. I’ve been experimenting with adding 20+ FAQs to the bottom of my funnels, both warm and cold. They should essentially function as pre-objection handlers (the most common objections raised on sales calls).

Understanding your offer fundamentally allows you to have super dialled FAQs.

Examples:

“What exactly is this?” Answer in one sentence.

“How does it work?” Two steps, no jargon.

“Why is it better?” One clear, specific advantage.

“How long until I see results?” Honest timeframe.

“What if it doesn’t work?” Your risk reversal.

Note: Actual FAQs should be more specific than this

Before/After examples across niches:

Coaches/Consultants

Before: “We’re an elite growth consultancy installing bespoke GTM systems to 10x your MRR.”

After: “We help B2B coaches get 5–10 sales calls a week by turning their best content into one simple offer and running ads to it. We set up the funnel and follow-ups so calls keep coming in without you posting more.”

Community Owners

Before: “We run a transformative Skool community with live calls, frameworks, and accountability.”

After: “We help your members hit one specific win in 30 days with a guided sprint. They get a checklist, a weekly call, and we track progress. You keep your community fees and make 3–5x more per member.”

Agency

Before: “Full-stack demand gen across omni-channel paid and lifecycle.”

After: “We write one page that explains your offer clearly and run ads to it. We follow up with everyone who clicks until they book a call. You get more calls from the same ad budget.”

SaaS

Before: “We offer end-to-end data-driven workflow automation with AI agents and LLM orchestration.”

After: “Our software watches your inbox, fills your CRM, and sends the next email for you. Your team spends time closing, not copying and pasting.”

Ecom

Before: “Premium performance nutraceuticals leveraging mitochondrial optimization and circadian science.”

After: “We sell a drink mix that stops afternoon crashes. Stir into water at 2 pm. Tastes great and helps you focus for 3–4 hours without jitters.”

Newsletter/Media

Before: “A multifaceted content business monetized through omni-channel brand partnerships.”

After: “We write one great email each week and sell the middle spot to a brand your readers already buy. They pay us, you get useful recommendations, and we stay free.”

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Mistake: Listing features, not the job.

Fix: State the one job customers hire you to do in their words.

Mistake: Vanity complexity.

Fix: Force the two-step mechanism. If you need step 3–5 to explain it, your packaging is wrong.

Mistake: Copying formulas without substance.

Fix: Add the why. Why is it faster, cheaper, safer? Be specific.

Mistake: Vague “for everyone.”

Fix: Name your who. “Gym owners,” not “businesses.”

Mistake: Hidden dread.

Fix: Name and remove the dreaded thing (time, tech, calls, risk).

Advanced: The 1-Page Feynman Sales Letter (for later today)

If you want to go deeper without getting fancy, build a 1-page doc (Google Doc is fine) that follows this exact narrative:

Line 1: The 10-word billboard.

Section 1: What it is (1 paragraph).

Section 2: How it works (two steps, add a simple diagram if you can).

Section 3: Why it’s better (one or two concrete advantages with a quick proof screenshot or number, or an Old Way – New Way graphic).

Section 4: Who it’s for (name the person and their current situation).

Section 5: What it costs and what happens next (one paragraph + one button to book/buy).

This “dynamic doc” style is perfect for fast iteration, showing on calls, and even potentially testing ad traffic before you overbuild a site.
